> **The central truth:** A road is more than a line on a map. It is a relationship maintained by the ground, the people who travel it, the signs they trust, and the records they leave behind. LEGEND's great evil begins when someone decides that a relationship is too dangerous to remain uncertain—and takes the power to dictate what everyone else must call true.

Everything below is a story proposal for the project owner to approve, revise, or reject. It is deliberately more detailed than player-facing lore. Players should discover this truth through journeys, documents, choices, and consequences rather than being told it in one exposition scene.

## 1. Legacy thematic premise — retain selectively

LEGEND is not secretly about maps alone. It is about **who gets to decide which version of the world other people must live inside**.

At the beginning, the mystery looks physical: a bell rings, a route marker has been moved, two records disagree, a crossing seems safe on paper but dangerous underfoot. As the series grows, the same conflict appears in more consequential forms:

- Who is allowed to travel safely, and who is made dependent on one approved road?
- Which account of a disaster becomes official, and which witness is called unreliable?
- Who owns a boundary when the ground, the law, and local memory disagree?
- What happens to a community whose name is removed from the records used by traders, officials, and travelers?
- Can a society coordinate without forcing every person to surrender their own evidence?

The Pale Surveyor wants to eliminate the conditions in which people must disagree, judge, and choose. The tragedy is that the original road network was created to help people survive disagreement. It worked best when no single keeper had absolute authority.

This gives the entire franchise a recurring dramatic engine:

**A practical system promises safety → its rules begin excluding inconvenient evidence → ordinary people are harmed by a decision that looks correct on paper → the player gathers independent evidence → the community must choose what to do with the truth.**

The enemy's methods can span the series, while the local conflict, cast, landscape, gameplay, and emotional resolution remain different from game to game.

## 2. The old road: what the magic actually means

### The principle of shared witness

**Proposed world rule:** In certain old places, a route can become unusually persistent when several independent forms of witness agree about it. A track across the ground, a marker at a junction, a record held by a keeper, and the repeated journeys of living people reinforce one another. The old builders learned how to use that relationship to make difficult routes more dependable.

This phenomenon need not be explained as a god, a sentient spirit, or a universal force that controls all magic. Old roadkeepers described it practically: a route was **held** when the world, the signs, the records, and the travelers continued to confirm one another. The underlying cause can remain partly mysterious.

The original network did not create the world from a map. It strengthened real, used connections that already existed.

### The Four Witnesses

These are proposed design principles for the old network, useful as clues and as a consistent system for future writers:

1. **Ground:** The physical route—stones, water levels, slopes, tracks, bridges, and actual conditions.
2. **Mark:** The visible sign that helps travelers recognize a route—cairns, cuts, mile-stones, lamps, boundary stones, and bells.
3. **Record:** The account that carries knowledge between journeys—ledgers, maps, dispatches, route notices, and archives.
4. **Passage:** Living people who have traveled the way, can report its condition, and can challenge a record with direct experience.

No single witness should be sufficient to establish that a route is safe. The first network was designed to compare them. Its warning bell did not choose a road; it announced that the witnesses no longer agreed and travel should pause until the discrepancy was investigated.

This rule turns the current quest chain into more than a string of matching symbols: the player is rebuilding a case from the ground, mark, record, and living testimony.

### What the corrupted network can and cannot do

To keep the supernatural frightening without making it limitless, the network should operate under stable constraints:

- **It needs a real anchor.** A correction must begin at an old node, marker, route, or linked record. The Surveyor cannot edit any place anywhere at will.
- **It needs agreement or compliance to spread.** A forged mark alone is not enough. The correction gains strength when records are altered, official procedures accept the change, and travelers repeatedly follow it.
- **It acts on connection before substance.** It can make a route loop, mislead a traveler, make a crossing difficult to reach, or cause a settlement to fall out of ordinary traffic. It does not create a mountain from nothing, resurrect the dead, or delete a person from physical existence.
- **It does not possess unlimited mind control.** Confusion, lost time, and gaps around a passage through a damaged node may occur. The Surveyor cannot simply command any person to believe any statement or rewrite all memories on demand.
- **It cannot make a falsehood permanently safe.** Floodwater still rises, stone still breaks, hunger still matters, and a road made “official” can still kill the people who travel it.
- **Independent witnesses weaken a correction.** A copy kept elsewhere, a traveler who remembers the old turn, a route marker left untouched, or two communities keeping separate accounts can preserve a contradiction the network cannot easily resolve.
- **The network is not the source of all supernatural events.** Monsters, strange lights, weather, human violence, and other mysteries may have their own causes. The Surveyor exploits a world that already contains danger; the Surveyor does not own every danger.

The strongest horror is not that reality can be rewritten without limit. It is that a limited system can make a false decision feel inevitable when every authority repeats it.

### The three stages of a correction

This is the recommended recurring pattern for designing clues and missions:

1. **Disagreement:** Ground, mark, record, and testimony conflict. The route may still be passable, but travelers should not trust it blindly.
2. **Ratification:** Someone replaces or suppresses the old sign, changes the record, or authorizes the wrong route. Institutional obedience gives the correction leverage.
3. **Entrenchment:** Enough travelers follow the new instruction that the old connection becomes harder to find, verify, and maintain. A community can be isolated without being physically erased.

A player should be able to interrupt this process at any stage. That creates meaningful investigative gameplay: find the older cut, preserve the first ledger, question the person who made the correction, or keep a route closed until a new inspection is actually completed.

### The Bell's job

The best fit for the existing story remains: **the Bell is an alarm, not a summons.**

One strike means, in the proposed old practice: *the witnesses disagree; do not let the signal alone decide your next step.* It is not an order to follow a blue light, a call to a chosen person, or a key that opens a secret door. The bell is valuable because it tells people when certainty has failed.

A single ring is therefore an ethical instruction as much as a plot clue: pause, compare, and verify.

Bell Fragments may preserve the bell's metal, sound, or old maker's marks, but they should not automatically become collectible magic keys that unlock the whole mystery. Their purpose can vary by object and scene.

## 3. The original failure: why the Surveyor became the enemy

### Before the title

The figure later called the Pale Surveyor was once **Aren Vey** (proposed name), a skilled routekeeper during an era remembered as the **Long Severance**. Wars, flood seasons, collapsed bridges, and rival local authorities had severed the old trade paths. Communities could be starving a few valleys apart because dispatchers did not know which reports to trust.

Aren helped build a shared road network from practical tools: route markers, warning bells, dated ledgers, redundant maps, and independent local keepers. The system was not originally meant to produce one perfect map. Its purpose was to let several imperfect sources correct one another before a caravan, patrol, or relief wagon committed to a dangerous path.

Aren was good at the work. The network saved lives. That part must remain true; otherwise the later tragedy has no moral weight.

### The Seven-Wagon Dispatch

**Proposed historical event:** During the Long Severance, seven relief wagons carrying food and essential supplies were sent through a contested pass to reach an isolated settlement. The central dispatch ledger still marked the route usable. **Ivara**, a local keeper, had witnessed a dangerous change in the ground and rang the warning bell. The evidence disagreed, the settlement was running out of supplies, and a full inspection would cost time the wagons might not have.

**Aren Vey** authorized the passage using the central record. The decision had a human logic under terrible pressure; it was still Aren's decision. The route failed, and the wagons did not arrive. No clear wreckage was recovered. Later witnesses disagreed about what they saw near the pass: some spoke of lights on a road no surviving map could locate, others insisted grief had turned an ordinary search into a ghost story. Keep those conflicting accounts alive in the early games.

The decisive wrong came afterward. The strongest working version is that Aren authorized or ratified an amended official account saying that no valid closure warning had been filed and that the intended destination could not be verified as an active settlement. Aren feared that admitting the full decision would destroy public trust in the network and leave other communities without relief; that fear explains the choice, but does not excuse it. Families who should have been able to demand answers were left with a ledger saying the dispatch had been carried out correctly. The cost was not only the lost wagons: it was the removal of responsibility and the beginning of a community's disappearance from public memory.

The full record would have shown a stale central report, Ivara's warning, Aren's authorization, and the consequences. The corrected record made the conflict vanish on paper while the people were still missing. This becomes the saga's defining wound: a human authority chose one witness and then removed the evidence that could challenge the choice.



### The wrong lesson

Aren could have accepted the truth: sometimes evidence conflicts, a decision must be made, and the person making it must remain accountable for the choice.

Instead, Aren drew the opposite conclusion: **a system that permits disagreement will always risk another Seven-Wagon Dispatch.**

Aren redesigned the network to resolve contradictions automatically in favor of a single master record. A local warning could no longer overrule the central line. Instead of asking whether the map was wrong, the system began to make the route behave as though the approved map must be right. At certain damaged nodes, rejected routes could fold into the Hollow Mile, leaving a person or a trace cut off from ordinary passage.

The change initially made travel more predictable. It also made dangerous reports easier to discard. Every time the network corrected a discrepancy, it appeared to prove that further correction was necessary.

Aren had found a way to prevent uncertainty from interrupting the system, but not a way to prevent bad decisions. The new design made the consequences harder to see and harder to challenge.

### The binding

When it became clear the system could not be completed within one ordinary lifetime, Aren chose to bind memory and intent into the oldest central node. The act was not a bargain with a demon and should not be explained as a sudden possession. It was a human attempt to guarantee that the rule would never be abandoned.

That act did not preserve Aren as an ordinary immortal person. It preserved the mission, the reasoning, the accumulated memories, and the compulsion to correct the record. Over centuries, the process has repeatedly preserved its goal while losing more of its capacity to understand the individual lives it affects.

This is the Pale Surveyor: not simply a ghost at the center of a machine, but a human decision that has become a system and still mistakes its own persistence for proof that it is right.

### Why “Pale”?

**Proposed reveal:** The oldest surviving diagram contains the outline of a routekeeper, but the name and identifying details have been scraped pale from the page. Later archivists used “the Pale Surveyor” as a description of the blank figure. It may have begun as an archival label rather than the title Aren used in life.

Do not use the name as an early-game monster label. Let it first appear as a damaged note, an anonymous figure in a copied diagram, or a title that different archives explain differently. The mystery is not just “who is the villain?” but “what was removed from the record, and by whom?”

### The one person who opposed the change

Aren's closest counterpart in the original network was **Ivara, a keeper of warning bells** (proposed name and role). Ivara believed that the system's purpose was to make disagreement visible before it became a tragedy. After the Seven-Wagon Dispatch, Ivara refused to certify a master record that hid the warning.

Ivara should not become a perfect prophet who understands everything. Ivara is practical, frightened, and capable of mistakes. The conflict is that Ivara insists that an imperfect warning must remain available even when it slows a decision.

The later historical record says little about Ivara. The player may eventually find evidence that Ivara separated records, copied testimony, or left independent instructions outside the central node. Whether Ivara was killed, went into hiding, or helped communities dismantle parts of the network is an intentionally open reveal. It should be chosen only when the games have earned it.

Aren's personal name and Ivara's surviving testimony create a deeper antagonist conflict without excusing Aren: the Surveyor is not fighting a heroic chosen one. The Surveyor is repeating a centuries-old argument with the evidence that its own system tried to erase.

## 4. The central contradiction that ties the whole franchise together

Aren's system does not simply hate lies. It hates **unresolved differences**. A lie that agrees with the master record can be accepted; an accurate warning that contradicts it can be rejected.

That distinction is essential. It makes the Surveyor more dangerous than a generic deceiver and gives the player a way to outthink it.

| The Surveyor's rule | The older roadkeepers' principle |
|---|---|
| One record must decide when witnesses disagree. | Contradiction means the route needs another inspection. |
| The old version creates confusion and should be removed. | The old version must remain visible so later people can understand the decision. |
| A safe system is one that never questions its own map. | A safe system can be challenged when the ground disagrees. |
| A community that cannot be located by the official map is an error. | The map exists to serve people; it does not grant them permission to exist. |
| The cost of a correction proves the need for more control. | The cost of a correction is evidence that the process must be reviewed. |

The Pale Surveyor should sometimes be factually right about a practical danger. Some local maps really are wrong. Some people do lie. Some routes should close. A central standard can genuinely save time and lives. The moral conflict is not “rules are evil”; it is that the Surveyor makes its rules impossible to contest.

The player should sometimes need the Office of Measure's expertise and still oppose its authority to make a disputed version final.

## 5. The human institution: the Office of Measure

The Office of Measure remains a useful working name for the modern institution attempting to standardize routes and records.

Its public mission is good: inspect roads, publish reliable routes, reduce fraud, coordinate relief, and keep merchants from exploiting contradictory toll maps. Its members include honest surveyors and dispatchers whose work genuinely helps communities.

The danger grows in layers:

- **Ordinary workers** want a road to be safe and their work to be trusted. They may notice a discrepancy but lack power to reopen a decision.
- **Local officials** want predictable supply, lower costs, and fewer disputes. They may choose the approved map because the alternative makes their responsibilities harder.
- **Beneficiaries** profit when a road is closed, a rival village is isolated, or trade is diverted. They use the Office's paperwork to disguise self-interest as procedure.
- **True believers** know the old network can impose corrections. They see the Surveyor's certainty as a cure for human failure.
- **Dissenters** believe standardization is useful but are unwilling to let one authority erase prior evidence.

The Office is not the Pale Surveyor's puppet in every scene. It should have real policies, internal disagreement, public accountability problems, budgets, careers, and people who can change their minds. The Surveyor's reach grows when the institution's legitimate systems create a path for old corrections to spread.

The franchise must preserve the distinction between *a useful standard* and *an unchallengeable standard*. The ultimate answer is not to abolish records, engineering, or coordination. It is to maintain them with visible provenance, independent witnesses, a right of appeal, and copies that no single authority can quietly overwrite.

## 6. The first game and the wider series

The first game, under the current product title **LEGEND: The Awakening**, is intended to travel through all planned regions: Ashmere, the Old Road, Greyfen, Ironpass, Blackwood, and the Ruined March. These are different acts within one continuous story, not separate releases. Each region dramatizes a new consequence of the same ancient failure.

- **Ashmere — trust:** the player has to decide whom to trust when the traveler cannot account for the last mile.
- **The Old Road — contradiction:** physical route evidence conflicts with markers and old instructions.
- **Greyfen — authority:** a crossing is marked open without a valid closure order, causing a local danger and exposing the difference between a record and an accountable decision.
- **Ironpass — dependency:** central standards genuinely protect travelers but can also make a settlement's survival depend on one corridor and one authority.
- **Blackwood — memory:** independent oral and family histories preserve names and paths missing from official records.
- **The Ruined March — erasure:** the Surveyor attempts the **First Accounting**, its first broad alignment in the present story; the player interrupts the immediate catastrophe but does not cure every loss or destroy the Surveyor's will.

The first game's climax should stop a concrete operation and leave visible consequences. Some routes reopen; some people remain missing; communities disagree about how to repair the network; authorities must answer for what they did. Do not end with a lore reveal that makes the player's local victories irrelevant.

Future titles revisit the same places at later points in the shared timeline. The next story should not be “the Ironpass game” simply because Ironpass is a distinct region. A later release may be set partly in Ashmere, Greyfen, and the March together, depending on the conflict and which characters are central. Each sequel needs a new dramatic problem built from the previous title's consequences—not just another node to disable.

The longer series can move through three broad thematic arcs, with exact title count left open:
1. **The missing:** families and communities pursue the truth about those taken or cut off by the Hollow Mile. The Surveyor exploits grief by promising their return.
2. **The reckoning:** Aren Vey's choices, Ivara's resistance, and the institutions that hid the losses are judged through several independent sources. Understanding the original wound does not absolve the harm.
3. **The final measure:** the Surveyor attempts to make one account permanent. The world must choose between centralized certainty and a dangerous but accountable network maintained by many local keepers.

Every future title should alter familiar locations, local rules, public memory, and relationships. Returning to a town should feel like returning to a place that lived through the last story.

## 7. The first game's clue architecture

The current clue chain is unusually well suited to this deeper story because its evidence changes in kind rather than merely repeating the same symbol.

| Existing beat | What it proves | What it must not prove yet |
|---|---|---|
| Dead lantern and the single ring | Something about the passage is unreliable; the traveler lacks a full account of the last stretch | That a god or the Surveyor personally summoned the traveler |
| The Moved Cairn | A physical marker can be shifted; a choice about what to do with conflicting alignments has consequences | That every moved marker is supernatural |
| Blackroot Bend seal | An older mark persists beneath newer work | Who made the newer cut |
| Greyfen Boundary Stone | A related mark appears in another place, with a changed route implication | That the same person physically altered both locations |
| Reedwatch Field Note | The newer cut existed by a particular date after the flood retreat was logged | That the note tells the full cause |
| Overwritten ledger and earlier weekly return | Official route status changed from “closed pending inspection” to “open” without an accountable correction | That the missing order definitely existed |
| Missing closure order | The authority for the change cannot be produced; absence itself becomes evidence | The Surveyor's name, identity, or full reach |

**Recommended next revelation:** The missing closure order may not simply be a document hidden in a drawer. The decisive finding could be that the weekly return was never sufficient authority to reopen the crossing. Someone converted a temporary status into a binding instruction, then allowed the paperwork to imply that the procedure had been followed. Keep the exact culprit open until the existing NPCs and route logic have been reviewed.

That reveals the saga's signature sin in a small, concrete case: a person takes a necessary warning, turns it into a convenient official certainty, then hides the act behind a clean record.

The next quest should therefore uncover **authority and accountability**, not another matching symbol. It can ask who was allowed to change the status, which independent copy survives, and why the local keepers were not notified. The Surveyor stays an inference, not the answer to every question.

## 8. Recurring characters as thematic anchors

The first game's characters already represent different kinds of responsibility. Use them to make the myth emotionally legible, while leaving room for their eventual arcs to change after the current game has been inspected and tested.

- **Mara Vell — preservation.** Her instinct is to compare sources and keep evidence. Her difficult question is whether an archive should preserve a harmful or humiliating truth when publishing it could endanger the living. The right answer should not always be “publish everything immediately”; provenance and consequences matter.
- **Old Brenn — accountability.** Ledgers make obligations visible, but clean columns can hide human decisions. Brenn can learn that a record is not trustworthy merely because it is tidy, signed, or old. He should be allowed to admit uncertainty without becoming incompetent.
- **Captain Oric — judgment under pressure.** Safety cannot mean blind obedience to the latest instruction. Oric can favor caution, reject a route, or protect people while still needing to explain who bears the cost of a closure.
- **The traveler — independent comparison.** The protagonist's importance comes from crossing social boundaries and placing evidence side by side. The traveler is not important because of prophecy, bloodline, hidden royal status, or immunity to the network.

These are proposed thematic directions, not new established backstories. A character arc should grow out of scenes the player can actually experience, not a biography pasted into the bible.

## 9. The series' repeated objects should change meaning

A durable franchise motif is not a logo repeated on every wall. The object needs to gain a new meaning as the player learns more.

- **The Bell:** First an eerie sound; then a practical warning; eventually proof that the old system was designed to pause a decision, not enforce one.
- **The Map:** First a tool for finding the road; then an apparently authoritative source; finally a claim that must be checked against the world.
- **The Ledger:** First a reward and trade record; then conflicting evidence; finally an ethical document that must preserve the decision and the prior version.
- **The New Cut:** First a strange symbol; then evidence of physical tampering; later the visible scar of a system that replaces rather than acknowledges an earlier truth.
- **The Blue Light:** First a danger or lure; then a sign associated with a failing node. Do not reduce every blue light, Lantern Wisp, or unexplained creature to a Surveyor machine unless a later story earns that specific answer.
- **The Missing Name:** First an archival mystery; then evidence that a person was removed from history; finally a test of whether the world can remember the person without allowing that identity to become another source of control.

Use motifs selectively. If every room contains the same mark, it becomes decoration and no longer tells the player anything.

## 10. The finale: a victory with a real cost

The Surveyor should not be defeated by discovering a secret weapon that overrides every other rule. The solution should use rules the player has already learned.

To interrupt the final alignment, allies must keep separate copies of the actual ground reports, old markers, and contradictory records alive across multiple locations. The bell is rung to warn travelers to stop trusting the master line. Local keepers refuse to destroy earlier versions, even when the old records contradict one another. The final action does not prove that one perfect map is correct; it proves that the network can survive when disagreement is visible.

The Surveyor's final argument should be persuasive in its own way: one map prevents wasted journeys, lost convoys, contradictory orders, and leaders who exploit uncertainty. The player cannot answer that suffering is unimportant. The answer is that safety without appeal becomes a different danger—especially when the system can conceal its own mistakes.

The victory has a cost. Centralized routes may become less convenient. Some connections must be inspected and repaired. Communities must maintain duplicate records and learn to challenge official instructions without rejecting useful expertise. Old roads remain dangerous. The world becomes more accountable, not magically perfect.

The Surveyor's defeat therefore changes the question. The world no longer asks, “How do we make every record agree?” It asks, “How do we act responsibly when the evidence does not agree?”

That is an ending with room for future stories. The central antagonist's plan is stopped; the human tendency to choose certainty over accountability remains a believable source of conflict.

## 11. Canon architecture for a long series

To keep continuity from fracturing across games, use three levels of story truth.

### Hard canon — preserve unless deliberately revised

- The old network began as a tool to make routes more dependable, not a weapon to erase people.
- The Pale Surveyor's central evil is the refusal to allow evidence to challenge a single approved version.
- The network has physical anchors and strict limits; it does not rewrite all reality at will.
- The Bell warns when the route witnesses disagree; it does not choose a destination for the traveler.
- Local people and local causes remain real. The Surveyor does not explain every monster, crime, flood, or hardship.
- The traveler has agency through evidence and decisions, not a mandatory chosen-one identity.
- The final solution cannot merely swap one absolute authority for another.

### Guarded mysteries — plan the answer, delay the reveal

- The Surveyor's original personal name and how the historical title “Pale” arose.
- Whether any recognizable part of Aren remains a conscious person, or whether the present will is an increasingly impersonal continuation of the original choice.
- Ivara's fate and what independent testimony survived.
- Which institutions knowingly helped the Surveyor and which were only exploiting the same procedures.
- The full original central node and what dismantling or reforming it costs.

These mysteries should have planned answers, even when their exact timing remains flexible. Avoid keeping everything vague forever.

### Flexible details — decide when production needs them

- The exact number and release order of games.
- The final names of ancient characters, institutions, nodes, and historical incidents.
- Which regional leader, trader, or official is responsible for a local action.
- The exact physical appearance or voice of the Surveyor.
- Individual character fates and local outcomes, provided they respect established events.

### Canon across player choice

Future games should preserve **shared historical truths** while allowing earlier choices to change local details. For example, the lower crossing may be restored, monitored, or kept closed by the community. What must remain true is that the player exposed an unauthorized correction and that surviving evidence showed the official status was not properly justified.

Each game's sequel-facing continuity packet should state:
1. The event that definitely happened.
2. The decisions that can vary.
3. The range of valid resulting states.
4. Which later scenes adapt to those states.
5. Which mystery remains intentionally unresolved.

If a later standalone game cannot import saves, choose one published reference timeline for fixed facts—but do not describe alternate player outcomes as wrong. They remain valid play experiences. For games that can carry state forward, preserve the variable details through flags or a clear continuity profile.

## 12. Rules for writers, designers, and future collaborators

Before an important reveal is added, check:

- **Causality:** What specific prior event made this possible?
- **Evidence:** Which two independent sources support the reveal?
- **Limit:** Why could the Surveyor not simply do this earlier or everywhere?
- **Cost:** Who pays for the correction, and who benefits?
- **Human motive:** What does the local person believe they are doing?
- **Counterplay:** What can the player discover or do that changes the outcome?
- **Payoff:** Which existing clue becomes more meaningful afterward?
- **Canon impact:** Does this change a proposed idea, or contradict an established fact?
- **Gameplay:** Can the player experience the information, not just read about it?
- **Restraint:** Does the scene leave enough ordinary life in the world for the mystery to matter?

No major reveal should exist only to make a wiki page deeper. It should change what a character believes, what the player can do, or how an earlier event is understood.

## 13. Current recommendation

Use the Pale Surveyor as a strong **proposed** long-range antagonist, but do not start by revealing the ancient history in the game. The next playable story should remain close to Greyfen: investigate the missing closure authority, show who could legitimately change a route's status, and let the player protect the surviving records from being collapsed into one convenient answer.

The franchise-scale story will become deeper because the small story is specific. One unsigned correction at one crossing should carry the same moral shape as the Seven-Wagon Dispatch centuries ago. Later, Ironpass, Blackwood, and the Ruined March can reveal increasingly consequential versions of that same choice.

**The saga's guiding line:** *A map should help people find their way. It should never be allowed to decide who is real, whose warning counts, or which loss must be forgotten.*


## 14. The Hollow Mile: the dark consequence of correction

**Status:** Proposed addition to the mythology; not established canon.

The Hollow Mile is the name later travelers give to the shadow-passage that can form at a damaged old-road anchor when the network forces a route to agree with an account that contradicts the ground or living testimony. It is not a new continent, an ordinary dungeon, or a portal the player can open on command. It is a failure of passage: a place remains physically real, yet the route that connected it to the rest of the world no longer reliably holds.

A person caught in a full correction might lose hours, return without the memory of a turn, hear a voice on a road with no one on it, or fail to come back. Some traces that persist along the abandoned path may be echoes. Others may be evidence that a living traveler is stranded somewhere the network no longer knows how to reach. In-world people call these cases “the Unreturned,” but this is a disputed term, not a precise scientific or religious category. Cold-blue lights may sometimes be remnants associated with such passages; not every Wisp or blue light is automatically part of the Hollow Mile.

The Surveyor's horror is partly that it believes it can reclaim every missing passage by making the records complete. It can preserve a route, a name, or an echo while still failing to restore the person who lived the journey. Its promise of rescue keeps people cooperating with a process that can take more people from their families.

### The rules that keep it coherent

1. The effect requires an existing old-road anchor and a correction under pressure; it cannot happen at arbitrary locations.
2. A correction must have a traceable human and institutional process: a marker is moved, a warning is suppressed, or an official order is accepted as final. The supernatural amplifies the act but does not erase responsibility.
3. It affects passage, access, and reliable memory around a linked route before it affects anything else. It does not create matter, resurrect the dead perfectly, or delete bodies at will.
4. Independent witnesses, preserved copies, physical landmarks, and local testimony can reveal the missing route and help interrupt a correction. They do not guarantee a rescue.
5. The player can rescue individuals or reopen a path, but each rescue requires investigation, access, and consequence. No one spell repairs every loss.
6. Keep ambiguity where evidence genuinely remains incomplete. A blue light may be a remnant in one scene and a separate phenomenon in another. Any classification should be earned through comparison.

### The intended dark themes

The Hollow Mile makes the Surveyor's ideology concrete: a government can fail to recognize a village; a family can be told that a missing person was never assigned to a route; a clerk can close a file while someone is still looking for the traveler. The supernatural horror grows from the human decision to make a record more real than the person it describes.

The tone should remain dark fantasy rather than empty grimness. A remembered name, a lamp kept burning, a road repaired by neighbors, or a witness finally believed is a real act of resistance. Hope is work, not a reset button.
