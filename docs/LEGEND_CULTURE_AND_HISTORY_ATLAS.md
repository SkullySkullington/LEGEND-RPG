# LEGEND — Culture & History Atlas

**Status:** Proposed worldbuilding expansion; not locked canon  
**Purpose:** Give each region a distinct history, civic life, set of customs, folklore, practical knowledge, and relationship to the road network. Make the world feel older and larger than the central mystery.  
**Companions:** [Series & Title Reframe](LEGEND_SERIES_CONTINUITY_REFRAME.md), [World & Story Bible](LEGEND_WORLD_BIBLE.md), [Deep Canon Foundation](LEGEND_DEEP_CANON_FOUNDATION.md), [Character Compendium](LEGEND_CHARACTER_COMPENDIUM.md), [Series Myth Arc](LEGEND_SERIES_MYTH_ARC.md).

> **Worldbuilding rule:** People do not live inside a plot. They live inside weather, work, debt, family, belief, memory, and place. The series mystery matters because it can disturb those things—not because every road, custom, and creature was secretly invented by the villain.

This atlas is an internal reference for writers and designers. Names, institutions, historical labels, and customs introduced here are proposals. Player-facing dialogue should reveal local practice through action and disagreement, not explain the entire atlas at once.

## CURRENT DIRECTION UPDATE — CULTURES BEYOND THE CENTRAL MYSTERY

Use [Fantasy RPG Direction: Monster Wars & the Awakening](LEGEND_FANTASY_RPG_DIRECTION.md) as the current main-story foundation. This atlas remains useful for distinct regional life, customs, economies, folklore, and local conflicts, but its proposed eras and route-network cosmology are not locked canon. The invasion and return of magic should affect each region differently without making every custom, creature, institution, or historical event a secret part of the same road-based conspiracy.

The Four Witnesses, Long Severance, Office of Measure, and related route traditions may survive as a local craft, belief, or disputed historical interpretation if they serve a region. Do not treat them as a universal religion or the true origin of all magic unless separately approved. Give each region monster-specific pressures, magical consequences, independent conflicts, and people whose lives are larger than the main plot.


## 1. The realm is connected, not unified

The lands of LEGEND are not one kingdom with one law, one church, one language, or one accepted history. They are a patchwork of towns, working settlements, mountain charters, old family claims, market agreements, and roads whose usefulness often matters more than the distant authority that claims them.

A traveler can cross three regions and encounter three different meanings of the word “safe”:

- In **Ashmere**, a road is safe when the gate watch has inspected the approach, a lamp remains lit, and a returning traveler can report what changed.
- In **Greyfen**, a path is safe only for a particular water level, load, and direction of travel. The same boardwalk can be sound in the morning and dangerous after a sudden rise.
- In **Ironpass**, a route is safe under stated loads, support conditions, weather, and convoy rules. A certificate that omits those conditions is not useful.
- In **Blackwood**, a route can be known through landmarks, seasonal changes, remembered accounts, and the testimony of people who live near it. A map without that local knowledge can be actively misleading.
- In **the Ruined March**, a road may be physically passable but politically dangerous because people dispute who may use it, who owns the surrounding land, or which settlement is recognized by the current registry.

No single region has the perfect solution. Local knowledge can be protective or possessive; central standards can prevent accidents or hide the people they disadvantage. The series gains depth when characters have reasonable grounds for different approaches.

## 2. The historical eras

The world should have history that predates and outlasts the Pale Surveyor. The following framework helps writers place ruins and traditions without forcing every old structure to belong to one network.

### The Loose Paths — before shared route standards

Most travel depended on family knowledge, seasonal paths, local markers, river crossings, and word carried by people who knew the country. Some routes were dependable for generations; others were forgotten when floods shifted, fires spread, wars closed a pass, or the households maintaining a path moved away.

Stories from this period describe pathfinders, ferrymen, signal fires, and travelers who left a stone or a carved branch for the next person. There was no single universal marking code. A similar mark in two regions may share a practical origin without sharing a hidden author.

**What survives:** Local place-names, boundary customs, old route songs, disputed tales of abandoned settlements, and a few markers whose original purpose has been lost.

**Design note:** Do not depict the pre-network era as ignorant or barbaric. It had sophisticated local knowledge; it struggled to carry that knowledge reliably between distant communities.

### The Marking — the first shared road practices

As trade and settlement grew, communities began agreeing on common marker shapes, dated route reports, safe resting places, and ways to record who had inspected a crossing. Bell signals and lanterns had different local uses, but the most useful practices gradually spread between carriers and roadkeepers.

This was not a single grand invention. It was a long, uneven process of borrowing, adaptation, argument, and repair. A village might adopt the new marker standard while retaining its own flood posts; a pass community might keep a bell code that lowland travelers could not safely interpret.

**What survives:** Different ages of stonework, repeated repair grooves, old dispatch terms, and route notices whose practical instructions outlived the people who wrote them.

**Design note:** The Old Road should contain layers. Some marks are old because a community has maintained them; others are old because nobody remembers who made them. Age alone does not equal supernatural significance.

### The Long Severance — the great interruption

**Proposed central event:** A long period of conflict, high water, collapsed roads, competing authorities, and delayed messages broke many connections between settlements. Relief wagons and dispatches were repeatedly forced to act on incomplete or contradictory information. A village could be near enough to reach but impossible to supply in time.

Aren Vey and other roadkeepers helped create a stronger network of independent reports, markers, dated ledgers, warning bells, and local inspection. Its original principle was redundancy: no single map or official should be the only witness when a route's safety is in doubt.

The Seven-Wagon Dispatch belongs to the later history of this era in the current proposal. Seven relief wagons were lost after Aren trusted the central record over local warnings. The account was then altered to imply that no valid closure warning existed. The tragedy's second wound was not only the lost wagons; it was the removal of the evidence that might have made the decision accountable.

**What is established within the proposal:** The network was originally protective, then its designer chose to force agreement rather than preserve contradictions.

**What remains open:** The exact dates, all participants, the number of affected settlements, how much of the official story was knowingly altered, and Ivara's role in preserving counterevidence.

### The Years of Agreement — when the network seemed to work

After the central route system began enforcing one approved record, many journeys became more predictable. Caravans followed fewer contradictory directions. Toll disputes became easier to resolve. Some dangerous shortcuts were closed, and some formerly isolated communities received regular supplies.

These genuine benefits helped the new order survive. A world that had recently suffered preventable losses was willing to accept the promise of certainty. People who benefited had little reason to question who was excluded.

Over time, the network's original safeguards were treated as inefficiency. Local copies became outdated. Independent warnings were moved to the margins. Some routekeepers' duties changed from checking the central record to ensuring that local practice conformed to it.

**What survives:** Convenient roads, old maintenance systems, records that are unusually uniform, communities that remember a period of safer trade, and unexplained absences from otherwise thorough accounts.

**Design note:** Do not make this era entirely evil. The network truly improved life for many people. Its failure came from its refusal to remain correctable.

### The Quieting — the loss of living oversight

**Proposed name used by later chroniclers:** The Quieting is the period when the older route system became fragmented, damaged, or difficult to maintain. Some nodes stopped answering; some communities abandoned central dispatches; others kept the forms while forgetting why the forms demanded independent checks.

The Surveyor's influence may have become intermittent as the network lost its local keepers. In some places, a route correction left only physical traces. Elsewhere, later officials copied procedures they did not understand, unknowingly making the old system easier to reactivate.

The name Quieting is disputed. Some regions remember it as peace after conflict. Others remember empty roads, quiet signal towers, missing trade, or official silence.

### The Present Rejoining — the age of restoration

Ashmere and Greyfen are rebuilding practical connections. Traders want predictable schedules; residents want the roads to remain locally accountable; officials want records they can defend. Modern efforts to standardize travel have legitimate benefits and real dangers.

The proposed Office of Measure emerges from this tension. It is not a surviving ancient cult with a uniform plan. It is a modern institution trying to repair a fractured system. A small inner group becomes dangerous when it discovers how old nodes can make its corrections affect more than paperwork.

**Present-day tension:** The Surveyor does not need everyone to worship it. It needs enough people to accept that the approved record is more authoritative than the evidence of the ground.

## 3. The Four Witnesses: an old craft, not a universal religion

The proposed Deep Canon Foundation describes four witnesses that the old roadkeepers once compared:

1. **Ground:** What the route physically is—water, stone, slope, tracks, bridges, erosion, and weather.
2. **Mark:** The sign that travelers use to recognize the route.
3. **Record:** The report that carries information between journeys.
4. **Passage:** The living testimony of people who actually traveled the road.

This framework began as a practical inspection discipline. It was not originally a mystical creed. Later generations turned parts of it into proverb, ritual, and folk practice, often without knowing the old technical rationale.

A roadkeeper saying from several regions survives in variants:

> “A mark may be moved. A page may be lost. The ground will speak to a boot—and the boot must speak when it returns.”

The line does not mean physical observation is infallible. A traveler can be mistaken, the ground can change after a report, and a witness can lie. The point is that no single form of evidence should be allowed to close a question permanently.

### The Four Witnesses in daily life

- Ashmere's watch may accept a gate inspection, but it still asks returning travelers what changed beyond the last lamp.
- Greyfen compares written flood levels with current water and the memories of residents whose homes lie along the low path.
- Ironpass engineers weigh a standard inspection against a foreman's report of a new crack in a support.
- Blackwood route-singers preserve the fact that a path was changed even when their song does not agree about why.
- The Ruined March's claim clerks compare land marks, family records, and testimony from displaced households rather than treating a single surviving paper as proof of a person's right to exist.

The tension across the series is not that evidence should always overrule rules. It is that a rule is only safe when there is a way to challenge it with new evidence.

## 4. Ashmere — the town that measures arrivals

### Its shape and atmosphere

Ashmere is a road-facing town built around the gate, the public lanterns, the square, work notices, repair shops, the inn, the Bent Lantern Pub, and the paths to the Old Road. It is neither a grand capital nor a forgotten village. It survives because travelers stop, local people maintain the road's edges, and the town can turn an expedition's risks into trade and useful knowledge.

Rain-dark stone, patched timber, smoke, lamps, wagon grease, wet wool, bread crust, and the smell of tools drying by a hearth give the town its character. People glance at the gate even while talking. They know the sounds of familiar wagons and can often tell by the return hour whether a caravan has delayed or failed to arrive.

Ashmere's warmth is practical rather than sentimental. A dry bench, a place to hang a cloak, a lamp kept burning, or an honest price can mean the difference between being welcomed and merely being allowed inside.

### How the town governs itself

**Proposed institution: the Lantern Table.** Ashmere's main public decisions are handled by a rotating gathering of representatives from the gate watch, regular carriers, market traders, repair workers, and the households that maintain the public lamps. Its authority is local and imperfect. Some decisions are made in meetings; others are settled by custom until a dispute forces the town to reconsider them.

The Lantern Table struggles with three pressures:
- The watch wants resources for immediate danger.
- Merchants want predictable access and the fewest delays.
- Residents want a voice when a route closure protects commerce but strands their household.

Oric has practical authority at the gate but does not own every decision about town spending. Brenn's ledgers can expose a cost but do not settle who should pay it. Mara's archive can preserve a previous decision but cannot make people accept it. These distinctions keep the established characters from becoming omnipotent officials.

### Customs and everyday manners

**Leave a Wick.** When a traveler arrives in bad weather, someone may leave a dry wick or a little lamp oil by the gate hearth. It is not a formal charity system; it is a custom that says the next person's arrival matters even if nobody knows their name. Wealthier households contribute routinely, while poorer ones may leave a twist of cord or a repaired match tin.

**Count the Wheels.** Carriers count the wheels, axles, and loads before a departure. It began as a simple safety practice and became a farewell ritual. A person who skips the count is seen as either hurried or trying to conceal something.

**The First Bowl.** At a household meal, the first bowl of broth is sometimes offered to whoever has come in from the road, even if a local laborer is hungrier. This custom is debated; some see it as hospitality, others as a habit that privileges strangers over residents.

**The Last Lamp Watch.** Before a long winter night or after a death on the road, residents take turns checking the public lamps. It is part maintenance rota, part remembrance. Children learn the route of the lamps before they are trusted to run errands after dusk.

**A debt spoken aloud.** If a person cannot repay a small debt on time, they may name the debt publicly at the market board and give a new date. It does not erase the debt, but open acknowledgement is considered better than disappearing.

### Local sayings

- “A warm lamp is a promise, not a wall.” Shelter matters, but it cannot remove the dangers outside.
- “Count the wheels before the miles.” Preparation is part of the journey.
- “A dry record has never carried a wet pack.” A person who records travel costs but has never traveled may be missing something.
- “Don't sell tomorrow's road for today's coin.” A short-term gain may ruin a future route.
- “The gate remembers who came home.” People take returning seriously; absence is not automatically proof of death.

### Ordinary conflicts that are not the central mystery

- The repair fund cannot cover every lamp before winter.
- A merchant wants to reserve all the dry wagon bays for paying customers.
- The pub is sheltering people who cannot afford rooms, putting its staff under pressure.
- A family disputes a debt that is technically recorded correctly but was incurred under an unfair contract.
- A courier wants responsibility before anyone believes they are ready.
- A cartwright must decide whether to repair an old design or recommend replacing it.

These conflicts should continue even when the central road mystery is quiet. The town feels real when the player sees how limited resources affect people who have no idea what the Pale Surveyor is.

## 5. The Old Road — a route with many makers

The Old Road is not one clean artifact left by an ancient civilization. It is a succession of trails, repairs, diversions, bridges, military detours, local shortcuts, flood changes, and partial restorations. Some stretches are older than any surviving written account. Others were laid within living memory and already have a half-dozen arguments attached to them.

A mile marker may have three generations of cuts because each generation repaired the route differently. A cairn can be moved for a legitimate reason—erosion, a changed ford, a damaged bridge—or for a dishonest one. The player's task is not to assume every alteration is sinister. It is to establish what changed, when, under whose authority, and with what consequence.

### Route etiquette

- Travelers warn the next person about hazards when the warning can be made clearly and safely.
- A marked shelter is not a guarantee that it is occupied or stocked.
- A signal may mean different things in different regions; the sender's origin matters.
- A route report should carry a date and the conditions under which it was observed.
- A traveler is expected to say when a path was abandoned or not fully inspected rather than marking it “safe” to avoid delay.
- Local people may refuse to give a precise shortcut to a stranger until they understand why it is needed. That caution can be reasonable, possessive, or both.

### The roadside offerings

Small pieces of cloth, bent nails, wax, twine, carved chips, and broken lamp glass sometimes appear by old markers. Their meanings vary. Some are practical repairs. Some commemorate a death. Some mark a promise, a safe return, a lost animal, or a place where a traveler gave up and turned back.

Do not make every offering part of a single ancient code. The road has collected generations of ordinary grief and ingenuity. The Surveyor's story is more powerful if it enters a world that was already rich with human meanings.

### The bell-mark

**Current story element:** An older bell-mark appears beneath a newer cut at more than one location. The evidence supports repeated alteration; it does not yet prove a common author.

**Proposed history:** Bells were once maintained as warning devices at selected route junctions, not at every mile. Their signals instructed keepers to pause travel when observations disagreed. The mark identified a place where a warning had been registered, not a place a traveler was supposed to follow.

The mark may eventually be found on tools, dispatch slips, or old training boards. Its presence should be rare enough to matter. Later stories may uncover related signs, but do not let the icon become a universal stamp on every old object.

### The dead lantern

A lantern can fail for mundane reasons: bad oil, wet wick, cracked glass, careless maintenance, or a deliberate hand. The dead lantern from the traveler's opening matters because of its position, timing, and connection to the single ring—not because dead lanterns are always supernatural.

The unanswered questions should survive several possible explanations. Someone may have maintained the lantern poorly; someone may have used it to signal; the old network may have failed nearby; or multiple causes may overlap. The player deserves evidence, not a predetermined mystical diagnosis.

## 6. Greyfen — a settlement that reads water

### Its shape and atmosphere

Greyfen is built around raised timber walks, stilted houses, shallow dark water, docks, peat stores, fishing platforms, board bridges, reed roofs, and weathered stone footings. People tie boats before they greet visitors. A door may have a high-water mark on its frame, and a house's repairs tell a story about which side takes the worst current.

Sound is part of the setting: rope pulled through a wet ring, water tapping under floorboards, a pole striking mud, a ferry bell, fish crates scraped over timber, rain on reeds, and the hollow knock that says a boardwalk joint has loosened. Greyfen should have warmth and commerce, not endless melancholy. Fish is smoked, nets are mended, children race along the raised paths, and market sellers argue over the quality of salvage.

### The High-Water Table

**Proposed institution:** Greyfen's public decisions are discussed at the High-Water Table, a rotating group representing the landing, household paths, fishers, peat workers, boat builders, salvage crews, and flood-record keepers. Its strength is firsthand knowledge; its weakness is that each group has a different idea of which loss can be tolerated.

The landing warden can close a path quickly in an emergency, but broader closures require negotiation because they affect food, work, access to family, and the ability to claim salvaged goods. The Table does not eliminate disagreement; it is meant to make the trade-off visible.

### Customs

**Mark the High Water.** At the end of a flood season, households compare the water marks on public posts. A strange year is marked as strange rather than folded into the normal line. Children may copy the marks as a lesson in how a record can change.

**Dry Knot, Wet Knot.** Ropes used for public walkways are tied with different finishing knots depending on whether they have been checked before or after a rise in water. Visitors often mistake the distinction for decoration.

**Share the First Catch.** In some neighborhoods, a household that lands the first catch after a storm sends a small portion to the families whose walkways took damage. It is not universal and sometimes causes arguments over who counts as a neighbor.

**Name the Missing Bank.** If a house or path is lost to the water, the name may remain in a work song for a season or longer. The custom allows people to mourn a place without pretending the water will return it.

**Do not trust a dry boot.** A friendly rebuke aimed at someone who makes a decision about the marsh without getting close enough to test it.

### Greyfen's economy

Greyfen survives through overlapping work: fishing, net repair, peat cutting, salvage, ferrying, boatbuilding, raised-path maintenance, storage, and the trade of goods recovered from places the water has covered. A bad season affects everyone differently. Fishers want boat access; peat workers need dry storage; salvage crews want freedom to recover goods; householders want stable paths; the landing needs enough traffic to pay for repairs.

A central road plan could help Greyfen send goods reliably. It could also privilege one landing and make other neighborhoods depend on it. This is why Greyfen's political story should grow from practical infrastructure rather than vague corruption.

### Local sayings

- “No dry boot knows a marsh.” Experience matters, but even experience must be checked against today's water.
- “The post tells you where yesterday stood.” A measurement is a record of a particular time, not a promise about tomorrow.
- “A boat is only shallow until it is full.” Capacity is a condition, not a fixed label.
- “Water keeps what wood forgets.” Flooded objects may preserve traces of lost places, but they do not tell the whole story alone.
- “Tie for the return.” A rope or plan must account for the journey home, not merely the exciting outward leg.

### Local stories beyond the Surveyor

- A salvage dispute over a chest containing household keepsakes and useful tools.
- A debate over whether to repair a walkway that serves few households but is the only route for an elderly resident.
- A new shallow boat that is safer in low water but too costly for smaller fishers.
- A missing verse in a high-water song, omitted at a family's request.
- Two waterline records that disagree because the posts were moved for legitimate repairs at different times.

The Surveyor may exploit poor records in Greyfen, but the region's conflicts exist without it. The marsh makes risk, memory, and maintenance visible; it should not be a blue-tinted version of Ashmere.

## 7. Ironpass — work measured in load and consequence

### Landscape and life

Ironpass is a mountain region of narrow ledges, cut steps, old mining galleries, timber supports, freight yards, switchback roads, snowmelt channels, and shelters built where a loaded wagon can stop without blocking everyone behind it. Its people understand that weight, weather, and time interact. A road that safely carries a mule train may not safely carry an ore wagon; a route marked open can still require a smaller load or an escort.

The region should be loud in ways Ashmere and Greyfen are not: hammering support braces, wind on high spans, metal wheels, winches, distant warning calls, and the long delay before an echo returns. Warmth comes from shared meals after a shift, gloves drying by a stove, and crews waiting for the last worker to leave a gallery.

### The Pass Charter

**Proposed institution:** Ironpass follows a Pass Charter that sets common duties for freight operators, mines, road crews, and the people responsible for inspection. A route certificate is valuable because it tells a convoy what was checked, by whom, and under what conditions.

The charter was created after a series of accidents caused by overloaded carts and rival companies blaming one another. Its regulations saved lives. The danger begins when an office starts treating the certificate as more real than the support, the load, or the worker who hears a crack.

### Customs

**Count the Load Twice.** The first count is made at departure; the second by a different person before a steep descent. It prevents theft and makes each count independently accountable.

**The Last Tap.** Before a crew enters a repaired tunnel section, someone taps a support and listens for the expected answer. It is a work practice, not a magical spell, although folk stories claim the mountain remembers careless crews.

**No Solo Certificate.** In honest practice, the inspector who prepares a safety report is not the only person who approves it. The custom exists precisely because skilled people can become overconfident.

**Share the Heat.** A convoy delayed by weather shares some fuel or hot food with the crews maintaining the pass. Refusal is legal but remembered.

### Social tension

The standard route helps mine owners promise reliable delivery and helps workers plan shifts. It can also turn a small community's livelihood into a single point of failure. A closure might protect a convoy but cut workers off from wages; a rushed reopening might keep wages flowing until the mountain collapses.

The player should hear credible arguments from workers, auditors, merchants, and local households. Maelin Rusk can be a sincere believer in standards because she has seen them prevent death. Dorr Ket can be equally serious about safety because she knows the written rule does not replace the person standing beneath the load.

### Local sayings

- “Stone hears the load before the clerk does.”
- “A certificate is a promise about a test, not a promise from the mountain.”
- “If the echo changes, stop before the next tap.”
- “No convoy is late until the last safe route is known.”
- “A shut pass can starve a village; an open pass can bury it.”

### Region story beyond the central mystery

Ironpass's central conflict can involve a production slowdown, an unsafe gallery, and a freight charter written to benefit larger operators. The Office of Measure's route records complicate the matter, but do not cause the mountain, invent the economic pressure, or control every person involved. The player's work should change how inspections are conducted and who can appeal a decision.

## 8. Blackwood — the forest that remembers by repetition

### Landscape and life

Blackwood is not simply a dark forest full of monsters. It is a working landscape of timber, charcoal, medicinal plants, hunting grounds, small clearings, old footpaths, woodcutters' shelters, and settlements separated by stands that look similar until someone notices the slope, the roots, the birds, or the way rain moves. The light is filtered rather than absent. Fog gathers differently in hollows; sounds travel unevenly; familiar trees fall and alter a route.

Blackwood's people maintain knowledge through repeated walks, family route songs, seasonal signs, notches in wood, and overlapping maps. A newcomer may call this unreliable. A resident may point out that a clean map becomes unreliable the moment an old tree falls or a logging crew changes the path.

### The Knot Councils

**Proposed institution:** Some settlements keep a Knot Council, an informal gathering of households responsible for maintaining local paths, stores, and seasonal records. Their knot-cords are practical mnemonic tools; a cord may record the order of landmarks, a warning, or a promise to repair a bridge. Different households may use different systems.

Knot Councils resist the idea that one official map can contain everything, but they are not automatically wise or fair. Family knowledge can become gatekeeping. A respected elder can use a tradition to protect their status. Local versions of a route may conflict because one family benefits from a timber boundary while another loses access.

### Customs

**Walk It Before You Name It.** A new path is not given an official local name until at least two people have walked it under different conditions.

**Two Names for One Path.** A route may keep an old family name beside a newer market name. The difference can record whose route it was before a trade agreement altered how it was used.

**The Fallen Tree Lesson.** When a landmark tree falls, the community does not simply remove it and pretend nothing changed. Children are shown the old path and the new one, learning why the old route description must be amended.

**Keep the Bad Verse.** If a route song contains a known mistake, the correction is taught beside the old verse. The mistaken line explains how earlier travelers went wrong and prevents later singers from assuming the two versions were always identical.

### Oral history without romanticizing it

Blackwood can preserve histories that centralized records lose, but memory is not magically truthful. Songs simplify. Families omit shameful events. A place-name may change because a community wants to distance itself from an old crime. Two accounts can both contain evidence and still disagree about responsibility.

Eda Thorne's role is to help the player compare versions, not to announce that oral history always wins. The region's practical wisdom is redundancy: a community that preserves several accounts can challenge an erasure, but it must still decide what to do with the contradictions.

### Local sayings

- “The tree moved; the story did not.”
- “A path has two ends and several owners.”
- “Sing the mistake where the child can hear it.”
- “Old does not mean true. Forgotten does not mean false.”
- “Ask who benefits from the short version.”

### Story beyond the Surveyor

A dispute over a timber boundary, a hunting ground, or a family accused of betrayal can reveal competing historical accounts. The Surveyor's system may threaten to collapse all versions into one, but the community still has its own conflicts over ownership, reputation, and whose memory deserves to be taught.

## 9. The Ruined March — the cost of an official border

### Landscape and life

The Ruined March is a frontier shaped by old conflict: broken towers, re-used stone, abandoned farm lanes, half-repaired bridges, claim markers, former military roads, and settlements built around what remains usable. The region is not one uninterrupted battlefield. It contains markets, harvests, repairs, weddings, and children who have grown up knowing ruins only as places to play or shelter animals.

The land's political past is visible because buildings and markers have been repurposed. A former boundary post may now support a roof. A military road may serve as the only safe path to a water source. A collapsed wall may mark the edge of a claim still argued about by descendants.

### The Claim Houses

**Proposed institution:** After the old conflict, local registrars and claim houses began recording who had lived where, which families had fled, and what evidence remained of ownership. Their work can restore rights, but it can also deny them if a family has lost its documents or if the current record favors the new ruler.

Len Aster's work grows from this system. Her challenge is not to abolish records; it is to prevent missing papers from becoming proof that a person never belonged.

### Customs

**Name the Road You Took.** A returning household recounts the route it used to come home, partly to help others and partly to affirm that the journey happened.

**Stone in the Threshold.** Some families place a small stone from their former home at a new doorway. It is a personal practice, not official proof of ownership.

**No Claim Without a Witness.** A land claim should include a person who can explain the relationship behind it, not just a line on a page. This can protect displaced families but also enable intimidation of witnesses.

**Keep the Old Boundary Visible.** Even when a new boundary is accepted, some communities leave the older marker standing nearby. The paired markers do not settle every dispute; they keep the history of the decision visible.

### The political wound

In the March, a map can be more powerful than a sword because it tells officials which road they will patrol, which tax they will collect, and which residents they are obliged to protect. When a village is missing from the recognized record, the consequences are material: trade ignores it, patrols bypass it, relief does not arrive, and its people must repeatedly prove that their homes exist.

This is where the Surveyor's philosophy becomes most dangerous. The system does not need to vaporize the village. It only has to make the institutions that matter stop acknowledging the route to it.

### Local sayings

- “A border can move without a boot crossing it.”
- “A burned paper does not burn the house it described.”
- “The road home is not a verdict.”
- “A witness is a person, not a seal.”
- “No map can carry the whole weight of a home.”

### Story beyond the Surveyor

The central conflict can involve competing claims to a farm road, a refugee register, and a community whose official identity is contested. Some claims will be fraudulent. Some will be honest and incomplete. The player should have to judge evidence and consequences rather than simply side with every displaced person or every established owner.

## 10. Belief, ritual, and the supernatural

There is no need to give the setting a single universal religion with a precise doctrine of every supernatural event. Different people pray, swear, mourn, bargain, or keep customs in ways shaped by work and family. Some believe in household spirits or a presence on the road; others treat the same actions as respect for the dead or old-fashioned caution.

The setting feels more believable when people have varied explanations for the same event:

- One traveler calls the cold blue light a marsh lure and avoids it because the path is unsafe.
- Another believes it is a sign left by someone who died off-road.
- A keeper thinks the lights appear when old signal equipment is damaged.
- A scholar compares reports and refuses to settle the question from one sighting.
- A local trader uses the rumor to charge more for safe passage.

These positions can coexist before the plot confirms anything. Do not make the most skeptical character automatically foolish or the most mystical character automatically wise.

### The quiet dead

Across the regions, families keep different practices for people who die far from home. Some leave a wick in a window. Some speak the route taken. Some keep an empty place at a meal for one season. Some write the person's name into the family record only after a witness returns. These are customs for grief and accountability, not proof of a single theology.

A missing traveler may be remembered in conflicting forms: a name in a ledger, a verse in Greyfen, a marker repaired at a pass, a small stone on a doorway. Those echoes allow the series to show how a person remains socially real after physical absence.

### The Lantern Wisps

**Current game element:** Lantern Wisps are part of the enemy and event vocabulary, and cold blue light appears along the Reed Cut.

**Canon boundary:** Their full nature is not established. Some appearances may be associated with old route infrastructure; others may arise from different causes. The world should not reveal that every wisp is a Surveyor tool by default.

A good wisp encounter should answer something observable—where it appeared, what it did, what tracks or residue remained—while preserving uncertainty about the underlying cause. Later evidence can distinguish types or reveal regional beliefs, but each new classification should be earned.

### Blackroot wolves

Blackroot wolves belong to the world's ordinary danger. Local claims that they fear firelight can be useful survival knowledge without making the wolves agents of the central evil. They may be frightened of flame, trained by previous encounters, or simply less likely to approach a lit camp. Do not turn all creature behavior into a clue about the Surveyor.

### Magical certainty should be rare

The road network's unusual effects remain bounded by the Deep Canon Foundation. Other magic may exist, but should have its own cost, history, and rules. A rune, a creature, a shrine, or a blue light must not acquire new powers solely to solve the current story's problem.

## 11. Calendar, seasons, and the texture of time

A common calendar may exist for trade paperwork, but local people often organize life around environmental cycles rather than abstract months. Until exact names and dates are needed for a quest, writers should use seasonal descriptions consistently instead of inventing a different named month in each document.

### Proposed seasonal markers

- **First Thaw:** Mountain melt begins to affect rivers and low bridges. Routes are inspected before full trade resumes.
- **Peat Rest:** Greyfen's cutting and drying cycle leaves some workers with time for repairs and some with too much rain.
- **High-Water Turn:** Greyfen households record the season's maximum water level and compare it with earlier years.
- **Long Dusk:** Ashmere checks public lanterns and restocks oil as daylight shortens.
- **Pass Quiet:** Ironpass reduces heavy travel during a period of unstable snow, stone, or runoff.
- **Rootfall:** Blackwood communities mark changes to familiar paths and inspect places where landmark trees have fallen.
- **The Home Count:** In the March, displaced families and local registrars revisit household and land records after the harvest.

These are proposed regional labels, not a finalized universal calendar. They are useful because each ties time to work, risk, and memory.

### How to date a historical clue

A clue should be dated in a form its author could realistically use: a week of watch duty, the second day after the flood retreat, the third freight count of a season, a harvest marker, or a public repair rota. A date should narrow a question rather than function as an arbitrary puzzle code.

For the current Greyfen evidence chain, “two days after the flood's retreat was logged” is effective because it compares two independent records. Future clues should use the same discipline: explain what event anchors the date, who recorded it, and what could make the date inaccurate.

## 12. Food, trade, and ordinary material culture

The series should describe food and goods by their source, storage needs, and practical use—not as a generic list of medieval props.

### Ashmere

- Dense bread and broth that can be reheated at a roadside camp.
- Dried apples, hard cheese, onions, beans, and salted meat for longer journeys.
- Lamp oil, wicks, leather patches, waxed wrapping, rope, and wheel fittings.
- Repaired coats and boots whose patches tell where the last journey wore them through.

The town's food culture favors travel-ready provisions. Sella's knowledge is the ability to prepare a meal that remains useful after a wet day and a bad camp.

### Greyfen

- Smoked or salted fish, roots kept in dry peat stores, reed-wrapped parcels, and foods designed to survive damp conditions.
- Rope, netting, shallow boat fittings, waterproof wrapping, salvage timber, peat bricks, and oils.
- Households keep tools above the last known floodline and use inexpensive materials for objects they expect to replace.

Greyfen's market should contain goods that make sense beside water. A luxury might be a dry storage shelf or a stretch of rope that has not stiffened with old salt and mud.

### Ironpass

- Dense shift meals, hot broth, preserved roots, hard cheeses, and stores that can withstand cold travel.
- Ore, worked iron, support timbers, pulleys, axle components, harness repairs, and measured cargo weights.
- Tools are marked by crew or work team because responsibility for a failed support matters.

### Blackwood

- Smoked game, mushrooms and roots gathered with local knowledge, charcoal, timber, resins, medicinal plants, and crafted hunting gear.
- Items often carry family repair marks. An object can have a recognized maker without being owned by the person who made it.

### The Ruined March

- Preserved grain, salvaged iron, reclaimed bricks, patched saddles, seed stores, and household items carried from an abandoned home.
- Some goods have more value as proof of origin than as materials. A damaged door hinge might show which house it came from, but only if someone recognizes the repair.

## 13. Names, speech, and literacy

Do not make all people from one region speak with a novelty dialect. Distinguish voices through profession, sentence habits, vocabulary, and assumptions rather than phonetic spelling.

- **Ashmere speech** tends to be concise about travel conditions, debts, and return times. A person may ask “Did you bring it back?” before “How was the road?”
- **Greyfen speech** qualifies statements by water level, load, direction, and recent weather. “Safe” is incomplete without a condition.
- **Ironpass speech** is explicit about measurement, load, tolerances, and responsibility. It may sound severe because an ambiguous instruction can kill workers.
- **Blackwood speech** often includes the source of a claim: “My grandmother's version,” “the north-path song,” or “the year the ash tree fell.”
- **The March** is careful with names, relations, and claims because those details can decide whether a person receives protection or loses a home.

Literacy varies by work and access. An unlettered ferryman can have better route knowledge than an educated official. A clerk can read a form perfectly and misunderstand the conditions it describes. These differences should be treated as expertise in different domains, not as a simple intelligent/ignorant split.

## 14. Factions and public institutions

### The Gate Watch — Ashmere

A practical watch, not a secret order. Its responsibilities include gate safety, basic traveler checks, patrols, and the first response to missing expeditions. Its authority is strongest at the gate and weaker on routes far from town.

**Failure mode:** Mistaking a consistent procedure for a complete understanding of the danger.

### The Lantern Table — Ashmere

The proposed civic forum that weighs public repairs, route priorities, and the town's share of travel costs.

**Failure mode:** Allowing merchants or established households to dominate meetings because they have more time, money, or paperwork.

### Greyfen's High-Water Table

The proposed local forum for flood response, raised paths, salvage questions, and competing livelihood needs.

**Failure mode:** A group with strong local knowledge can still protect its own neighborhood at another neighborhood's expense.

### The Old Roadkeepers — historical profession

The roadkeepers were once a broad craft tradition, not one secretive brotherhood. They inspected markers, compared route reports, kept public signals, and taught travelers how to report conditions. The old network was created from this craft.

The profession's name may survive in different regions, but duties and standards diverged after the network's central authority became unreliable.

**Failure mode:** Every profession can become defensive when its authority is challenged.

### The Office of Measure — proposed modern institution

A modern body of surveyors, dispatchers, inspectors, and officials working to standardize routes and records. It promises fewer accidents, fairer tolls, reliable freight, and clearer responsibility.

Its work provides real benefits. Most members need not know that the old network can affect route access. A small inner group learns to synchronize old nodes and treats contradictions as obstacles to be removed.

**Internal positions:**
- Field inspectors who want reliable standards but accept appeals.
- Administrators who prize uniform records because they make work easier to manage.
- Commercial beneficiaries who profit from the routes the Office approves.
- True believers who think the Surveyor's corrections prevent human failure.
- Dissenters who know the old methods are harming people but fear the alternative.

**Failure mode:** A process created to distribute responsibility becomes a process that allows each person to say the decision belonged to someone else.

### Blackwood's Knot Councils

Local groups that maintain seasonal routes and pass on family or settlement knowledge. Their authority comes from repeated use, craft, and community memory rather than a central charter.

**Failure mode:** Local tradition can be used to exclude newcomers or hide a family's misconduct.

### The Claim Houses — proposed institutions in the Ruined March

Registrars and local offices that record property, household histories, and claims after displacement.

**Failure mode:** A record intended to protect a person's rights becomes the only proof the authorities will accept of that person's existence.

## 15. Folktales: three stories with different versions

These tales can appear as dialogue, songs, a scratched carving, or a child repeating a version learned at home. Their first purpose is cultural texture. Any connection to the Surveyor must emerge later through evidence, not be assumed.

### The Lantern That Waited

**Ashmere version:** A tired traveler once found a lantern lit at every turn and reached town before the storm. The next winter, the keeper left one lantern unlit to see whether people had learned to look at the ground. The people who made it home were the ones who stopped when the light ended.

**Pub version:** The lantern keeper was simply drunk and forgot the oil.

**Moral people draw:** Light is a help, not a replacement for judgment.

### The Boat With Two Names

**Greyfen version:** Two sisters built a boat and gave it different names: one name for low water, one for high water. They argued until a flood proved both names necessary. The boat carried their neighbors because each sister knew a different part of the crossing.

**Merchant version:** The sisters could not agree on a name, so the boat never received a proper registration.

**Moral people draw:** Different descriptions can be useful, but the practical conditions still have to be understood.

### The Path That Forgot the King

**Blackwood version:** A king ordered a road cut through the forest and demanded that every tree be marked. The woodcutters made the road but kept the old paths. When the marked road washed away, the king's messengers were lost, while the families who remembered several ways still reached one another.

**Official version:** The king built an expensive road without hiring competent maintenance crews.

**Moral people draw:** Authority cannot maintain every part of a landscape by declaring it maintained.

These are not direct prophecies of the Pale Surveyor. They are local stories that give players a framework for thinking. Different versions should sometimes reveal more about the storyteller than about history.

## 16. Rumor design: how locals talk about events

Rumors need a source, a motive, and an uncertainty. Avoid filler that just says “strange things are happening on the road.”

A useful rumor pattern:

- **Ashmere pub rumor:** A carrier swears a marker was facing the wrong way after rain. The speaker wants an escort contract renewed and may be exaggerating.
- **Gate rumor:** A watch member reports that three returning travelers gave the same route description but disagreed about where a lamp stood. The watch member is worried about safety, not trying to explain the supernatural.
- **Greyfen market rumor:** A salvage claim was rejected because a recovered crate was not listed in the old household ledger. The dispute is about ownership, not magic.
- **Ironpass freight rumor:** The Office's new route schedule reduced delays for the largest convoy but lengthened every small operator's journey. The figures may be accurate while the conclusion is contested.
- **Blackwood way-house rumor:** Two families sing the same path verse with different names for the same bend. One version was changed after a feud.
- **March registry rumor:** A village appears on tax records from one decade and vanishes from the next. It might be clerical neglect, political erasure, war, or a road that became unusable.

A rumor should provide either a lead the player can check, a glimpse into local values, or a clue to a relationship. Not every rumor needs to be true, but the player should be able to learn why someone believes it.

## 17. The canon boundary: what belongs to the Surveyor, and what does not

The central antagonist is stronger when it is responsible for a defined kind of harm. The following distinctions should be maintained:

### Likely connected to the old route network, if the proposed arc is approved

- The repeated correction pattern beneath newer marks.
- Certain route records that conform too neatly despite physical evidence to the contrary.
- Some local failures of passage around linked old nodes.
- Old warning practices associated with the single bell.
- Modern procedures that unintentionally strengthen the network's authority.

### Not automatically connected

- Every flood, landslide, mine accident, crime, debt dispute, or missing traveler.
- Every cold-blue light or Lantern Wisp.
- Every bad official decision.
- Every old ruin or marked stone.
- Every lie in a ledger or every version of a folk song.
- Every person who supports central standards.

A story can begin as an ordinary human problem and later reveal a specific connection, but that connection needs evidence and a causal explanation. The main antagonist must never become a blanket answer to everything the world contains.

## 18. A chronology for writers

This is a relative chronology, not a finalized year-by-year calendar.

| Period | Broad event | Evidence likely to survive |
|---|---|---|
| The Loose Paths | Local routes and knowledge are maintained by families, ferrymen, and settlement customs | Songs, place-names, reused stones, fragmentary family accounts |
| The Marking | Communities share more common route practices and dated reports | Marker standards, inspection notes, early ledgers |
| The Long Severance | War, floods, broken roads, and contradictory dispatches cut settlements off | Relief records, lists of missing caravans, competing local memories |
| The Seven-Wagon Dispatch | Under the current proposal, Aren trusts the central route record over local warnings; the wagons are lost and the report is altered | Copies of a dispatch, a bell-keeper's testimony, an amended account |
| The Years of Agreement | The corrected network makes travel more predictable for many communities | Standardized maps, repeated forms, evidence of real trade and safety gains |
| The Binding and Quieting | Aren's will is bound to the old central node; local oversight weakens and some connections fail | Incomplete diagrams, unusually uniform records, abandoned maintenance stations |
| The Present Rejoining | Modern officials work to standardize and repair route systems | Current inspection reports, duplicate ledgers, physical marks beneath new cuts |

The historical order of the Seven-Wagon Dispatch and the Binding is proposed. Exact dates, durations, names of settlements, and how much the public remembers should be locked only when a game has a specific use for them.

## 19. In-world fragments for an immersive voice

These are sample artifacts for tone and world texture, not final game dialogue.

### From an Ashmere lamp-maintenance notice

> The public lamps are not to be judged by their appearance at noon. Check each wick after rain, note the oil used, and mark repairs before the next watch. A lit lamp is evidence of maintenance, not proof that the road beyond it is clear.

### From a Greyfen flood record

> Water reached the second notch before dusk. The landing was dry at first count and slick at second count. Jessa says the south post was replaced after the eel baskets went missing; do not compare this mark with the older one until the join has been inspected.

### From an Ironpass freight charter

> The route is approved for the listed load under the stated conditions. Approval does not excuse a driver who ignores a changed support, nor a supervisor who orders passage after a new warning. Any change in load or ground requires a new count.

### From a Blackwood path song

> First the ash tree, then the split rock, then the water that runs east in spring. If the ash has fallen, do not walk the old verse as written. Sing the fallen line as well, so the next one knows why you turned.

### From a Ruined March claim register

> No surviving paper confirms this household's first tenancy. Three witnesses place them in the lower houses before the wall fell. The claim remains unsettled; the absence of a deed must not be entered as proof that the household was absent.

These fragments should be distributed across actual locations, objects, and quests. An in-world document earns its place when it reveals a person, a practice, a conflict, or a consequence—not merely when it sounds ancient.

## 20. Using the atlas in gameplay

Every region should express its culture through what the player actually does.

- **Ashmere:** Maintain a lamp, settle a fair debt, deliver a message, repair a cart, or choose which public route receives scarce materials.
- **Greyfen:** Compare flood marks, recover salvage with contested ownership, test a shallow boat, choose which walk is repaired, or preserve a version of a song whose omission protected a living person.
- **Ironpass:** Recheck a load, interview workers about a support, appeal a route certificate, or discover that a technically correct inspection no longer matches the current conditions.
- **Blackwood:** Walk a path under different conditions, compare two versions of a song, investigate a boundary maintained by rival households, or preserve the mistake that explains an older route failure.
- **The Ruined March:** Reconstruct a household claim, decide whether to publish a vulnerable witness's account, repair a road whose ownership remains contested, or help a registry preserve an unresolved claim without discarding it.

These examples are proposals. Reuse existing quest, discovery, route, journal, and NPC interaction systems rather than inventing a new subsystem for every cultural detail.

The player does not need to know the complete history to enjoy an errand. The purpose of the deeper lore is that a side quest still feels like it belongs to a wider world and may be remembered differently by a character in another region.

## 21. Continuity rules for this atlas

- Preserve the established setting of Ashmere, the Old Road, Greyfen, Mara, Brenn, Oric, and Sella.
- Keep new names, institutions, historical eras, and customs proposed until deliberately approved.
- Do not make every town share the same government, belief, vocabulary, or approach to safety.
- Do not confuse a local custom with a magical rule.
- Give every ruin a mundane history as well as any possible mystery significance.
- Let people remember past events selectively, and give them reasons for what they preserve or omit.
- Make travel and trade shape social life; never use a road as a purely abstract line between quest hubs.
- If an institution from the legacy route-network proposal is retained, make its useful work real enough that its policies and limits can be understood; do not assume the Office of Measure exists in current canon.
- Let local knowledge be powerful but fallible, and official standards be useful but challengeable.
- Ensure at least half of local conflicts remain unrelated to the central awakening or any single antagonist.
- Resolve the immediate personal or regional conflict at the end of a story even when a larger mystery remains open.
- Reveal major historical claims through more than one source, including at least one source that complicates the first interpretation.

The long-term aim is a world in which players can recognize Ashmere from how its people welcome travelers, Greyfen from how its residents talk about water, Ironpass from how it measures risk, Blackwood from how it preserves paths, and the Ruined March from the cost of making a claim. The central threat should become more frightening because it threatens those distinct lives—not because every place turns out to have been designed for the same villain.

**The world's governing principle:** Routes connect places, but customs tell us what those places value. A shared world should preserve both connection and difference.


## 22. Recurring geography across titles

All planned regions described in this atlas are intended to belong to the first LEGEND game's shared geography. They are not a release plan. Ashmere, Greyfen, Ironpass, Blackwood, and the Ruined March should return in future titles with visible changes to their routes, institutions, customs, relationships, and memory of earlier events.
